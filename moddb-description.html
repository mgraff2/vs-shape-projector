Set it down, dial in a figure, and it casts a luminous guide onto the ground. Circles, rings, ellipses, rectangles, polygons and spirals, in layers around one shared centre, each able to lie dead level or lie on the land and follow the floor down as you dig. It places nothing and breaks nothing. It only shows you where, so the round tower is actually round and the moat closes on itself without a block being counted by hand.

Installs on Client and server. The server owns the figures, your client draws them, so everyone playing needs it — and everyone sees the same guides.
Game version Vintage Story 1.22.x, tested against every patch from 1.22.0 to 1.22.7.
Other mods Tested alongside Carry On, Chisel Tools, Farseer and Claims Radar, each on its own and all together, every release.
What it writes Nothing in your world. The guides are light: no substance, no collision, and the projector never places or removes a block.
Getting one Craft it from four cupronickel or brass ingots, two cupronickel plates, two Jonas parts, a clear quartz and a temporal gear. Place it, right-click it, and the dial opens.
Keys O hides every projection for you alone, rebindable. Inside the dial: Page Up stacks a copy of the selected layer a course higher, Page Down copies it a block outward, + and − resize every layer at once.

The problem it solves

Circles are the obvious one. The real difficulty is anything that has to be concentric, level, or laid on ground that is not flat — a moat whose two banks stay a fixed distance apart, a wall that repeats the same figure thirty courses up, a path that hugs a hillside. That is arithmetic you would otherwise do with a tape of blocks and a great deal of recounting.

Six shapes, forty-eight layers, one centre

Shape What you give it
Circle A radius, in half-block steps.
Ring Two radii. It draws both circles and leaves the band between them empty — that band is what you dig.
Ellipse A radius each way. Equal, and you have a circle.
Rectangle Width and depth, edge to edge, with the drawn size always shown so parity never surprises you.
Regular polygon Sides, a corner distance and a rotation. Three sides is the triangle; many is very nearly a circle.
Spiral Turns, the gap between laps, and where it starts.

Every layer shares the projector's centre and keeps its own size, thickness, height, colour, height offset and on/off switch.

What each part does

Click a heading to open it.

Layers and the shared centre (concentric by construction, not by counting)
  • Up to forty-eight figures at once, all drawn around one centre. Concentric is the default state rather than something you achieve.
  • The centre moves in half-block steps — on a block for an odd diameter, on the seam between two blocks for an even one. That single choice is what usually goes wrong when circles are laid by hand.
  • The centre need not be the projector. Offset it as far as you like and the instrument stands beside the work: in open water, inside a wall already standing, anywhere the middle of the figure is inconvenient to occupy. The dial shows the resolved centre, and a marker inside the miniature says where the projector itself sits within the design.
  • Add, remove, duplicate, reorder. Reordering is list order only — every setting travels with its layer, so nothing in the world moves.
  • Switch a layer off to hide it without losing it. Its settings are kept, and a global resize still adjusts it, so a hidden layer never falls out of step with the rest.
Thickness and height (one layer instead of twenty)
  • Thickness marks a figure several blocks broad in one layer, instead of stacking several layers a block apart. The band grows inward, so the outer edge stays exactly where the radius puts it — radius 11 at thickness 3 runs from about 9 out to 11.
  • Height stands a figure several blocks tall in one layer, so a thirty-block round tower is one layer rather than thirty. The figure repeats on that many levels going up from the layer's own offset.
  • Height obeys the ground. In Follow terrain each column is measured from its own surface, so a wall of even height rises over uneven land instead of levelling out.
  • Build feedback checks every level separately, so a half-built wall turns green as far up as it is finished.
Level, or laid on the land (the two ways a layer can stand)
Fixed height One dead-level line whatever the ground does — wall tops, floors, platforms. Where the earth swallows the line, cut down to it; where it hangs in air, fill up to it. That, too, is information.
Follow terrain The line sits on the surface like a rope laid out by hand, and follows the floor down as you excavate. The dig line is never lost halfway through the digging, and it always marks the present floor's edge.
Water Yours to rule, per layer. The line can rest on the water's surface and mark the bank, or look through it to the bed and mark the bottom.
Unloaded ground Columns whose land has not loaded yet stand at fixed height until it does, rather than guessing.

Circles, rings and spirals start out following the terrain, being usually of the landscape; rectangles and polygons start level, being usually of the building. Either may be changed on any layer.

A worked example: the moat (three layers, one centre, nothing counted)

Set the projector at the centre of a round holding and give it three layers.

  1. A ring, inner radius 20, outer radius 23, following the terrain. This is the moat, three blocks wide, both lines lying on the land wherever it goes. Dig between them; as the pit deepens the lines follow the floor down and the edge is never lost.
  2. A circle, radius 11, following the terrain. The path that rings the house, resting on whatever ground it crosses.
  3. A circle, radius 30, at fixed height with an offset to the wall's intended top. Level all the way round — hanging in air where the ground falls away, buried where it rises. Build up to it.

Three figures, one centre, concentric by construction. Nothing was counted.

While you build (feedback, the miniature, presets, shortcuts)
  • Marks turn green as you fill them. Place a block where a guide stands and its mark goes green, so the figure completes in front of you and what is left is obvious at a glance.
  • A holographic miniature floats above the block: the whole design in small, world-aligned so north in the mini is north in the world. Read a forty-layer plan without walking it. It can be switched off on its own, without stopping the projection, and server owners can set its size, height and style.
  • A model of your land. Switch on Model surroundings and the miniature also shows what already stands around the projector — ground, buildings, the walls under their eaves, water — every block in its real colour, with the figures drawn in their true places among it. A model of your home and of the work at once. Hide the figures and it is a survey of the land alone; switch off the world marks and the projector is hologram-only.
  • Fill up to level. A layer can mark every block from the ground up to its level under each column of the figure, so a pit under a platform shows exactly the fill it needs and a causeway can start at the lake bed.
  • Full detail at any size. Marks are drawn as merged faces, so a radius 256 disc at full thickness renders whole — in the world and in the miniature — with grid lines along the block boundaries so every mark stays countable.
  • Your colours. Twenty-four to pick from, each shown as itself beside its code, or type an exact hex value; and a mark opacity per projector.
  • Two looks. Faces draws marks as merged surfaces with grid lines, fast at any size; Blocks draws every mark as its own little cube, the classic look. A marks counter in the dial turns red when a figure passes the comfortable threshold for the look you chose — a warning, never a cut.
  • Freeze it. One switch stops all live updates for a projector, so a big figure can stand in a base as a centrepiece and cost nothing but its draw.
  • Named presets are saved per player, not per projector, so a shape you worked out once follows you to every projector you can edit. Load one in place of what is there, or add it on top of it.
  • Shortcuts for the repetitive part. Page Up copies the selected layer one block higher — a course per press, thirty presses for a thirty-block tower. Page Down copies it one block outward, filling toward a solid disc. + and − grow or shrink every layer at once, including the hidden ones, so nothing drifts out of step.
  • Tooltips that do the arithmetic. Hover any control and it tells you what it will do to the layer you have selected — "radius 11 → 12", "Y offset +2 → +3", "width and depth both +2, one block on each of the four sides" — and a greyed-out button says why it is greyed out.
  • A resize that would shrink a layer past its minimum leaves it alone and says so, rather than silently doing something else.
  • O hides every projection for you alone. Other players still see theirs.
  • It remembers. Break a configured projector and replace it, or carry it with Carry On, and it keeps its layers.

What the Shape Projector will never do

  • Place a block, or break one. It marks and nothing else. That is a decision rather than a limitation: a mod that only draws can never flatten the thing you were standing on.
  • Get in your way. The guides have no substance and no collision. You walk through them, build through them, and the block you place is exactly the block you placed.
  • Follow you around. A figure belongs to the projector that casts it. Take the projector away and the guide goes with it.
  • Guess. Where the ground has not loaded, a draped line stands at fixed height and says nothing rather than inventing a surface.

Good to know

  • Outlines are recomputed only when something actually changes, and a draped line re-resolves just the columns you dug — not the whole figure, every frame.
  • The green "done" tint is for level layers. A draped line follows the surface, so it can never be filled in.
  • The surroundings model is a view from outside: roofs, walls with their doorways and windows, the walls under eaves, water as its surface — never the inside of a closed room.
  • The handbook carries the full explanation in-game: the centre and its offset, layers, the two ways of standing, and the moat worked through.
